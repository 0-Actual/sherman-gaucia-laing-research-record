# Slavery, coercion and historical continuity

The owner’s hypothesis is: “It is my personal opinion that the slavery today is worse than it was a hundred years ago.”

Economic exploitation and coercion persist, and selected institutional continuities are documented. The recovered data support a rise in global estimated forced labour between 2016 and 2021. They do not provide comparable U.S. or global measures for 1926 and 2026 sufficient to establish the broader century-long claim. Legal abolition and subsequent protections are material changes; implementation and enforcement must be assessed separately.

## Evidence by population and period

### SL01 U.S. chattel slavery population baseline

The census enumerated legal chattel slavery. This is a U.S. count from 166 years before the review, not the requested 1926 baseline.

[Census Bureau, Abraham Lincoln and the census](https://www.census.gov/about/history/stories/monthly/2014/november-2014.html) — Opening population paragraph

### SL02 Abolition with a punishment exception

Ratification abolished slavery and involuntary servitude while retaining a criminal-punishment exception. This changed legal status; the exception and subsequent penal institutions require their own review.

[National Archives, Thirteenth Amendment](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/13th-amendment) — Ratification date and Section 1 transcript

### SL03 Money paid to institutions for prison labor in the 1923 survey

BLS reported this amount for hire under those two systems. Contractor payments to institutions differ from workers' earnings. The survey recorded no lease system in the institutions canvassed; its population does not cover all county practices or clandestine coercion. Its appendix also reproduces distinct state laws.

[BLS Bulletin 372, Convict Labor in 1923](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/convict-labor-1923-4005/fulltext) — Printed pages 2–3, Introduction; original published January 1925; searchable transcription is OCR

### SL04 Documented post-emancipation institutional continuity

The Library of Congress describes how criminalization, arrests and fees supplied convict leasing after emancipation. This supports a specific institutional connection to coercive labor, rather than an inference from a present person's ancestors or school history.

[Library of Congress, The Convict Leasing System](https://blogs.loc.gov/inside_adams/2021/06/convict-leasing-system/) — June 17, 2021 article body, excluding reader comments

### SL05 Global forced labour and forced marriage estimates

ILO, Walk Free and IOM estimate 49.6 million people in modern slavery in 2021: 27.6 million in forced labour and 22 million in forced marriage. Global 2016–2021 comparisons are supported with methodological qualifications; finer comparisons have additional limits. These are not a 2026 count, a U.S. census or a measure of ordinary employment, debt or inequality.

[Global Estimates of Modern Slavery, 2022](https://www.ilo.org/sites/default/files/2025-09/ILO_GEMS-2022_Report_EN_Web.pdf) — Printed pages 20–22 and methodological annex pages 109–113; PDF hosting date does not change observation year

### SL06 Profits from coercive labor

ILO estimates private-economy forced-labour profits; the 2014 comparison is inflation-adjusted. This is an estimate of exploitation proceeds, not lobbying expenditure, corporate revenue or profit attributable to the AI companies in this study.

[ILO, Annual profits from forced labour](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/annual-profits-forced-labour-amount-us-236-billion-ilo-report-finds) — March 19, 2024, global profit and inflation-adjusted comparison paragraphs

### SL07 Trade law changed its treatment of forced-labor imports

The Trade Facilitation and Trade Enforcement Act removed the consumptive-demand exception to the Tariff Act's forced-labor import prohibition. This is evidence of a legal change, not evidence that forced-labor goods ceased entering supply chains or that a particular donor bought the change.

[CRS, Forced Labor Trafficking in the International Fishing Sector](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IF/PDF/IF10368/IF10368.4.pdf) — February 29, 2016, Recent Trade and Customs Legislation

- Compare like geography, population, legal/economic definition, observation year and measurement method before comparing counts or rates.
- Do not divide a present global estimate by an historical U.S. count and call the result a change in slavery.
- Treat the severity of harm, prevalence, total people affected, legal status and profits as separate dimensions. No universal worse score is calculated.
- Use public biography, adult roles and speeches only to establish a documented connection. Childhood, shared ancestry and institutional membership do not by themselves establish motives, coordinated action or culpability.
- Historical oppression and present exploitation can be established without asserting that all people in a racial, religious or economic group share guilt.
