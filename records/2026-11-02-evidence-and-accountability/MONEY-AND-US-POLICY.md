# Money and U.S. policy from 1776 to 2026

Sherman Gaucia Laing · Word.Quantum.Earth · Research edition, 1 November 2026. Assistance: Gaucia Actual — Root (Codex), G Man, Worship and Praise.

Financial interests have influenced U.S. policy through several documented routes: credit and treaty bargaining, protection of property interests, paid access, official appointments, government contracts, settlements and corrupt exchanges. Their evidentiary strength differs. A disclosed payment alone does not establish that a law was bought; an admitted exchange or judicial finding supports a much more specific conclusion.

This casebook starts with Revolutionary financing and follows selected mechanisms into contemporary corporate influence. Twelve primary-source cases and one historical context case establish concrete examples, rather than a continuous account of every transaction over 250 years. ## Trace each case

### CONTEXT1776 French assistance and the choice of commercial alliance

**Payer:** French Crown, routed through Beaumarchais and Roderigue Hortalez and Company. **Recipient:** American military supply effort.

**Money:** French livres, historical nominal amount 1,000,000 — Initial covert support described by Library of Congress; institutional historical synthesis, not a newly reconciled payment ledger.

American need for overseas military supplies shaped negotiations over commercial terms and alliance. Congress adopted the Model Treaty on September 17, 1776; agreements with France followed in 1778.

**Instrument and outcome:** Model Treaty of 1776 and Franco-American commercial/alliance treaties of 1778 Financing and diplomacy interacted. This is foreign state support of the Revolution, not domestic campaign finance or proof of a purchased vote.

**Evidence:** Institutional historical synthesis linking finance, procurement and diplomacy; exact accounting status disputed in historical records.

**Public roles and history:** actor role: Vergennes: French foreign minister; Beaumarchais: commercial intermediary; American commissioners negotiated assistance. continuity boundary: Specific state-credit and treaty relationship, not evidence of an unbroken secret control structure through 2026.

**Counterevidence and limits:** The State Department history records disagreement over whether early aid was a gift or loan. France also pursued strategic interests against Britain. Do not add repeated descriptions of the same funds or assume a dollar conversion. 

Sources: [Library of Congress, The Franco-American Revolution Part 1](https://blogs.loc.gov/international-collections/2021/06/the-franco-american-revolution-part-1/) — Paragraph on initial clandestine aid and the one-million-livre trading firm; [Office of the Historian, The Model Treaty 1776](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1776-1783/model-treaty) — Opening and congressional instructions paragraphs; retired historical series; [Office of the Historian, U.S. Debt and Foreign Loans 1775–1795](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1784-1800/loans) — Early French aid and disputed gift/loan status; retired historical series.

### EARLY01 Plantation and shipping interests in slave-importation bargaining

**Payer:** No political transfer established. **Recipient:** No political payee established.

**Money:** No payment amount established — No paid or promised political transfer quantified.

Madison records General Charles Cotesworth Pinckney defending slave imports through plantation output, shipping, consumption and revenue, and warning of lost South Carolina assent. Rutledge also tied consent to protecting imports. [E01]

**Instrument and outcome:** Constitution Article I section 9; Article V Congress could not prohibit covered importations before 1808. The text allowed a duty up to $10 per person; that ceiling is neither a verified collection nor a bribe. [E02]

**Evidence:** Direct recorded economic-interest argument and enacted constitutional protection; no check-to-vote trace.

**Public roles and history:** documented: The debate expressly connects enslaved labor, rice-region demand and carrying trade to political bargaining. [E01] actor role: Pinckney and Rutledge: South Carolina convention delegates. continuity boundary: Protection was time-limited. These records identify historical interests, not modern descendants' liability, childhood causes or a continuous corporate ownership chain.

**Counterevidence and limits:** Mason opposed the trade; Sherman prioritized forming the Union despite disapproval. Economic incentives and constitutional bargaining were interwoven. [E01] 

Sources: [Madison's Notes, Constitutional Convention, August 22, 1787](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/debates_822.asp) — Discussion of Article VII section 4: Mason; Ellsworth; General Pinckney; Rutledge; web lines 23–49; [United States Constitution, original text](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/constitution-transcript) — Article I section 9 clause 1; Article V protection of the clause until 1808.

### EARLY02 Creditor claims and federal assumption of state war debts

**Payer:** United States, through statutory debt funding. **Recipient:** Eligible subscribing state-debt certificate holders.

**Money:** USD 21,500,000 — Nominal statutory subscription ceiling, not cash paid.

Hamilton opposed reducing purchasers' claims to their acquisition cost, arguing that transferable claims, contracts and borrowing terms supported public credit. Congress authorized exchange of qualifying state war-debt certificates for federal obligations. [E03,E04]

**Instrument and outcome:** Funding Act, chapter 34, sections 13–15, 1 Stat. 138 Federal funding terms protected eligible certificate holders and assumed defined state liabilities. The $21.5 million ceiling was payable through certificates, not a $21.5 million cash lobbying fund. [E04]

**Evidence:** Strong fiscal distribution and creditor-policy connection; paid lobbying or a purchased vote not established.

**Public roles and history:** documented: Revolutionary war financing generated claims traded between original holders and purchasers; the report distinguishes foreign contractual debt from domestic restructuring. [E03] actor role: Hamilton: Treasury Secretary advising Congress. continuity boundary: Public-credit institutions connect finance and policy; no specific merchant/plantation fortune or modern successor ownership is traced here.

**Counterevidence and limits:** Hamilton presents the alternative of favoring original holders who sold cheaply, and argues against it on contract and public-credit grounds. [E03] 

Sources: [Alexander Hamilton, Report on Public Credit, January 1790, in Treasury reports](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/annual-report-secretary-treasury-state-finances-194/report-public-credit-january-1790-report-a-national-bank-december-1790-5629/fulltext) — Printed pages 5–10, discussion of original holders versus purchasers and transfer security; OCR lines 634–695, 833–917; conclusion at 1219; [Act making provision for the debt of the United States](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-1/pdf/STATUTE-1-Pg138.pdf) — Sections 13–15, printed pages 142–143 (PDF pages 5–6); state-certificate subscription ceiling and exchange terms.

### EARLY03 Yazoo land interests, legislative corruption and purchaser protection

**Payer:** Georgia Company purchasers. **Recipient:** Georgia treasury; legislators allegedly promised land interests.

**Money:** USD 250,000 — Georgia Company statutory purchase terms: $50,000 before grant plus $200,000 due November 1, 1795.

The complaint alleged Gunn, M'Allister and Walker promised legislators land shares for favorable votes. Georgia's repeal preamble separately declared corruption and participating legislators' purchase interests. [E05,E06]

**Instrument and outcome:** Georgia sale act of January 7, 1795; repeal of February 13, 1796; Fletcher v. Peck (1810) Repeal attacked the grants. The Court protected an innocent subsequent purchaser's vested rights; it did not adjudicate each alleged bribe. [E06]

**Evidence:** Legislative corruption finding plus specific judicially recorded allegations; neither an audited bribe total nor a criminal verdict.

**Public roles and history:** documented: Land-company ownership incentives reached legislative allocation of territory. The repeal contrasted $500,000 accepted consideration with an $800,000 rejected offer. [E05] actor role: Named purchasers, Georgia legislators, governor and later courts. continuity boundary: Speculation-to-law mechanism established at this scope; specific plantation profits, later heirs and current beneficiaries not traced.

**Counterevidence and limits:** Peck disputed the promises and asserted lack of notice. Judicial reasoning protected purchasers rather than endorsing corruption. [E06] 

Sources: [Georgia rescinding act preamble, reproduced in American State Papers: Public Lands, volume 1](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/llscd/llsp028/llsp028.pdf) — Printed page 144, paragraph beginning with affidavits and proofs; comparison of rejected $800,000 with accepted $500,000 and legislators' purchase interests; [Fletcher v. Peck, 10 U.S. (6 Cranch) 87](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/10/87) — Reported paragraphs 5–10, 18–25 (including $50,000 and $200,000 terms), 148–158 and 189; web lines 38–108, 570–607, 713.

### EARLY04 Bank publicity spending and failed federal recharter

**Payer:** Second Bank of the United States. **Recipient:** Printers/publication vendors; complete named payees unavailable in selected account.

**Money:** USD 80,000 — Approximate expenditure reported by Jackson for 1831–1832, not an independently audited exact total.

Bank-funded circulation of favorable articles, congressional reports and speeches aimed at public opinion and charter renewal; the March 1831 resolution described public information about the bank. [E07]

**Instrument and outcome:** Recharter bill veto; executive deposit-removal policy statement Jackson vetoed the bill on July 10, 1832. Its proposed $3 million bonus/$200,000 annual payment was not enacted by that bill. [E08]

**Evidence:** Direct reported institutional advocacy spending; recipient-level accounting and vote causation incomplete.

**Public roles and history:** documented: A chartered bank used institutional resources to defend privileges; the veto discusses benefits to domestic and foreign stockholders. [E08] actor role: Bank president/board; Jackson as President. continuity boundary: Institutional publicity is a comparable mechanism, not proof that a modern bank inherits this entity's acts or that Jackson's accusation is neutral accounting.

**Counterevidence and limits:** Spending did not secure the charter. Jackson's adverse account also identifies independent charter-expiry and administrative reasons for deposit removal. [E07,E08] 

Sources: [Andrew Jackson, Message Read to the Cabinet on Removal of the Public Deposits](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/message-read-the-cabinet-removal-the-public-deposits) — Board resolutions November 30, 1830 and March 11, 1831; 1831–1832 expenditure paragraph; web lines 88–98. Alternative removal rationale at line 55.; [Andrew Jackson, Bank veto message](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/19th_century/ajveto01.asp) — Opening return of bill to Senate; constitutional/state-rights objections; proposed $200,000 annuity and $3 million bonus, web lines 23–32 and 104–106.

### USW01 Providence Tool: contingent compensation for a federal musket contract

**Payer:** Providence Tool Company. **Recipient:** Norris, procurement intermediary.

**Money:** USD 75,000 — Norris's claimed compensation; company disputed the amount; payment not established.; USD 13,500 — Trial judgment for Norris, subsequently reversed; not a verified disbursement..

The Court's record attributes procurement to Norris's efforts under a contingent agreement and describes influential introductions to Secretary of War Simon Cameron.

**Instrument and outcome:** July 1861 War Department contract: 25,000 muskets at US$20 each. In 1864 the Supreme Court held the compensation agreement unenforceable on public-policy grounds and remanded for a new trial.

**Evidence:** Primary documentary record; evidentiary qualifications stated with the outcome

**Public roles and history:** Company: Rhode Island corporation supplying arms. Norris: intermediary. Cameron: Secretary of War.

**Counterevidence and limits:** The rule rested on the agreement's tendency, without requiring proof of improper means. This is neither a proved bribe to Cameron nor proof that the musket contract itself was canceled. 

Sources: [Tool Company v. Norris, 69 U.S. (2 Wall.) 45 (1864)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/69/45/) — Statement of facts, pp.45–48; opinion, pp.53–56..

### USW02 Crédit Mobilier: benefits to James Brooks within a subsidized railroad enterprise

**Payer:** Thomas C. Durant / Crédit Mobilier; later issuance arranged through Sidney Dillon. **Recipient:** Charles H. Neilson, named holder; House committee found James Brooks the beneficial owner.

**Money:** USD 10,000 — Brooks paid for 100 Crédit Mobilier shares transferred December 26, 1867.; USD 5,000 — Union Pacific bonds accompanying that transaction; stated securities amount, not cash.; USD 20,000 — Union Pacific stock accompanying that transaction; stated securities amount, not market value.; USD 5,000 — Price of 50 additional shares issued to Neilson February 29, 1868; financed by Dillon. Committee described their value as US$15,000–20,000; repayment unresolved..

The committee found the additional shares secured Brooks's official favor. Brooks and Alley denied the alleged promise to influence the Democratic side; the committee relied on surrounding circumstances too.

**Instrument and outcome:** Pacific Railroad Act, July 1, 1862, §§1, 5–6: Union Pacific charter and repayable federal bond aid. February 27, 1873: House censured Ames and Brooks; Brooks vote 174–32. The adopted resolution condemned use of his offices to obtain stock.

**Evidence:** Primary documentary record; evidentiary qualifications stated with the outcome

**Public roles and history:** Brooks: Representative and government director of Union Pacific; Dillon: Crédit Mobilier president.

**Counterevidence and limits:** The committee did not find corrupt intent in the other previously discussed House purchasers. Later share transactions cannot establish that they caused the earlier 1862 statute; no specific subsequent roll-call purchased by Brooks's benefits is identified here. 

Sources: [Congressional Globe, February 18, 1873: Poland committee report](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc30903/m1/796/) — Printed p.1464: opening findings and 'James Brooks, of New York' passage.; [Hinds' Precedents, Volume 2, Chapter 42](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-HPREC-HINDS-V2/pdf/GPO-HPREC-HINDS-V2-16.pdf) — §1286, printed pp.853 and 856–857: proposed findings and adopted censure resolutions.; [Pacific Railroad Act, July 1, 1862, 12 Stat.489](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-12/pdf/STATUTE-12-Pg489.pdf) — §1, pp.489–490; §§5–6, pp.492–493: US$1,000 bonds, sixteen per mile, repayment conditions..

### USW03 Fort Sill trading appointment: Evans–Marsh–Belknap payments

**Payer:** John S. Evans → Caleb P. Marsh → William W. Belknap. **Recipient:** Marsh, intermediary; Belknap, Secretary of War.

**Money:** USD per year 12,000 — October 8, 1870 Evans–Marsh contract, payable quarterly; later terms could change.; USD 1,500 — Receipt by Belknap on or about November 2, 1870, alleged in Article IV, specification 1..

House articles expressly tied payments to appointment and retention; Article III alleged Marsh passed half to Belknap.

**Instrument and outcome:** Appointment of Evans as Fort Sill post trader on October 10, 1870, and continued tenure. August 1, 1876: Senate acquitted on every article because no guilty vote reached two-thirds.

**Evidence:** Primary documentary record; evidentiary qualifications stated with the outcome

**Public roles and history:** Evans: trader; Marsh: intermediary; Belknap: Secretary of War.

**Counterevidence and limits:** Charges are not a conviction. The official compilation records 22 not-guilty votes based on jurisdiction and three on evidence. Annual rates changed; no extrapolated multi-year total is asserted. This concerns administrative patronage, not proof of purchasing a statute. 

Sources: [Hinds' Precedents, Volume 3, Chapter 77: Impeachment and Trial of William W. Belknap](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-HPREC-HINDS-V3/html/GPO-HPREC-HINDS-V3-26.htm) — §2449, Articles I, III, IV specification 1 and V; §2467, final votes and acquittal..

### USW04 Doheny–Fall: US$100,000 and Elk Hills oil leases

**Payer:** Edward L. Doheny, through his son. **Recipient:** Albert B. Fall, Secretary of the Interior.

**Money:** USD 100,000 — Delivered November 30, 1921; demand note given; no principal or interest repayment reported in the opinion..

The Supreme Court sustained findings that Doheny corruptly secured Fall's influence and official action; Fall collusively dominated negotiations.

**Instrument and outcome:** Pan American contracts of April 25 and December 11, 1922; Naval Petroleum Reserve No.1 leases of June 5 and December 11, 1922. February 28, 1927: cancellation upheld; contracts and leases held illegal and void.

**Evidence:** Primary documentary record; evidentiary qualifications stated with the outcome

**Public roles and history:** Doheny controlled the two Pan American companies and served as Petroleum Company's president, then board chairman.

**Counterevidence and limits:** Companies argued loan, not bribe. Civil rescission did not require statutory criminal bribery or government financial loss. This opinion is not a criminal conviction of Doheny. Elk Hills is the California transaction, not Sinclair's Wyoming Teapot Dome lease. 

Sources: [Pan American Petroleum & Transport Co. v. United States, 273 U.S.456 (1927)](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-supreme-court/273/456.html) — pp.485–486 (instruments); pp.493–494 (payment); pp.498–500 (findings and limits); pp.500–503 (authority and disposition)..

### US46_01 Tobacco Master Settlement Agreement: lawful payments exchanged for specified claim releases and public-health restrictions

**Payer:** Original participating manufacturers Philip Morris Inc., R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co., Lorillard Tobacco Co. and Brown & Williamson; subsequent participating manufacturers also assumed obligations.. **Recipient:** The settling governments, allocated under the agreement; no claim that attorneys general received the money personally..

**Money:** About US$206 billion through 2025.

The Illinois appellate court describes dismissal and release of specified government claims in exchange for annual payments and marketing concessions. Pennsylvania's official summary documents youth-targeting and advertising restrictions, document access, and limits on opposing youth-access measures.

**Instrument and outcome:** Master Settlement Agreement, implemented through state litigation and consent-decree enforcement. An enforceable settlement changed participating manufacturers' conduct and settled government claims. This is direct money-for-defined-legal-consideration, not evidence that companies bought a federal law.

**Evidence:** PRIMARY_LEGAL_BARGAIN: judicial account of the agreement, official state enforcement summary, and contemporaneous Congressional Research Service analysis.

**Public roles and history:** Manufacturers negotiated with state attorneys general as defendants and public litigants. The legal bargain and payment machinery, rather than personal ancestry, establish the relationship.

**Counterevidence and limits:** The broader federal McCain tobacco bill failed on June 17, 1998. CRS distinguishes that proposal from the narrower MSA. Pennsylvania's summary expressly preserves criminal prosecution; the agreement was not general criminal immunity. No inference that all tobacco regulation was purchased. The US$206 billion is a dated projection/obligation description, not a present receipt reconciliation. Individual and federal claims must not be silently folded into the settling governments' releases.

Sources: [Illinois Appellate Court, People v. Lorillard Tobacco Company, No. 1-06-2326, March 30, 2007](https://www.illinoiscourts.gov/resources/b17736a1-9b7b-4e53-baaf-f1b0ef051bad/1062326.pdf) — Printed pages 2–4: parties, November 23, 1998 bargain, escrow, auditor and adjustments; [Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General, Summary of Key Points in the Master Settlement Agreement](https://www.attorneygeneral.gov/the-office/divisions/public-protection/tobacco-enforcement/master-settlement-agreement-summary/) — Youth targeting; outdoor advertising; lobbying restrictions; no criminal immunity; public document access; states' recovery; [CRS RL30058, Tobacco Master Settlement Agreement (1998): Overview and Issues for the 106th Congress, November 5, 1999](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metacrs891/m1/1/high_res_d/RL30058_1999Nov05.html) — Summary and Overview. ### US46_03 Tigua–Scanlon–Abramoff: paid access and a failed casino amendment

**Payer:** Ysleta del Sur Pueblo, commonly called the Tigua tribe. **Recipient:** Scanlon/CCS and Abramoff; Ney's corrupt benefits are separately documented, not an allocation of the full tribal fee..

**Money:** US$4,200,000; About US$1,850,000.

The Senate traces paid fees and concealed sharing to an unsuccessful reopening plan. Ney separately admitted attempts to insert four client-requested amendments into HAVA in exchange for benefits.

**Instrument and outcome:** Requested non-election amendment to election-reform legislation to permit the Tigua casino to reopen. The Tigua casino-reopening effort failed despite the payments. Ney's September 15, 2006 announcement concerned a signed agreement to plead guilty; it was not yet the court's entry of that plea.

**Evidence:** PRIMARY_DOCUMENTED_FAILED_INFLUENCE: Senate investigation using financial records and testimony, plus admitted corrupt official conduct in signed plea documents.

**Public roles and history:** Abramoff was a federal lobbyist and Scanlon his concealed business partner. Ney chaired the House Administration Committee from 2001 to January 2006, a relevant legislative access point.

**Counterevidence and limits:** The tribe did not know of Scanlon's secret payment to Abramoff. It must not be labeled a knowing participant in that concealed arrangement. Ney's broader stream of benefits is not an exact allocation to this amendment. Do not add unrelated clients, total travel costs or other casino projects. Promised access did not guarantee passage.

Sources: [U.S. Senate Report 109-325, Gimme Five—Investigation of Tribal Lobbying Matters, September 5, 2006](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CRPT-109srpt325/html/CRPT-109srpt325.htm) — Tigua summary; Chapter V, Operation Open Doors and conclusion; fee table and US$1,850,000 secret share; CCS accounting discussion citing Abramoff plea paragraph 20; [DOJ, Congressman Robert W. Ney Agrees to Plead Guilty to Charges Involving Corruption and False Statements, September 15, 2006](https://www.justice.gov/archive/opa/pr/2006/September/06_crm_622.html) — Signed agreement, committee role, approximately 2000–April 2004 conspiracy, benefits and attempted four HAVA amendments.

### US46_02 Cunningham: contractor payments exchanged for federal appropriations and contract intervention

**Payer:** Several defense-contractor coconspirators; DOJ separately identifies Mitchell Wade and Brent Wilkes. The aggregate is not assigned entirely to either man.. **Recipient:** Representative Randall Harold 'Duke' Cunningham.

**Money:** At least US$2.4 million; More than US$1 million; the cited DOJ description does not supply a complete cash/noncash allocation.

Cunningham admitted that the benefits were exchanged for official action influencing appropriations and contract execution for contractor owners. The Attorney General identifies Wade's firm's receipt of federal contracts as resulting from illegal payments, and describes Wilkes' contract-steering relationship.

**Instrument and outcome:** Federal appropriations and execution of defense contracts; the selected releases do not identify every appropriation line or award number. Recipient pleaded guilty November 28, 2005 and received a 100-month sentence March 3, 2006. This supports a proven procurement/appropriations corruption mechanism, not a price for all U.S. defense policy.

**Evidence:** ADMITTED_CRIMINAL_EXCHANGE: DOJ's account of the plea and sentence, corroborated by the Attorney General's account of contractor convictions.

**Public roles and history:** Cunningham was a sitting congressman; the benefiting coconspirators were majority owners of defense contractors. Their official procurement relationship is material. No childhood or family-life inference is used.

**Counterevidence and limits:** The prosecutions and sentence show institutional detection and sanction. No selected evidence establishes that every associated contract lacked legitimate deliverables or that every campaign contribution by contractors was corrupt. Do not publish an unsupported US$150 million award total or a precise beginning date from these sources. A quoted bribe-menu schedule is not an audited list of completed payments and is not used as the case total. Later clemency or appellate developments were not assessed in this bounded historical mechanism review.

Sources: [U.S. Attorney, Southern District of California, Former Congressman Cunningham Sentenced to More Than 8 Years in Prison, March 3, 2006](https://www.justice.gov/archive/tax/usaopress/2006/txdv06cas60303.1.pdf) — Printed pages 1–2: sentence, November 28 plea, at least US$2.4 million and admitted appropriations/contract exchange; [Attorney General Michael B. Mukasey, Commonwealth Club remarks, March 27, 2008](https://www.justice.gov/archive/ag/speeches/2008/ag_speech_080327.html) — Paragraphs on Wilkes, Cunningham and Wade immediately before discussion of the Abramoff investigation.

### US46_04 FirstEnergy and the Householder enterprise: nuclear bailout enacted, protected, then partly repealed

**Payer:** FirstEnergy Corp. and related energy companies in the Householder scheme; the scheme aggregate is not allocated entirely to one payer legal entity by these releases.. **Recipient:** Generation Now and the enterprise supporting Larry Householder's political power, allied candidates and personal benefits; not US$61 million paid solely into Householder's personal account..

**Money:** Nearly US$61 million.

DOJ reports bribe-funded electoral support, Householder's official assistance in passing HB6, and efforts defeating the repeal initiative. FirstEnergy separately admitted payments for specific nuclear-legislation action in its July 22, 2021 deferred prosecution agreement.

**Instrument and outcome:** Ohio House Bill 6 nuclear bailout and effort to defeat the referendum seeking its repeal; later House Bill 128. HB6 passed and the referendum was defeated. HB128 repealed the nuclear-credit payment and corresponding customer-charge provisions, effective June 30, 2021. Householder was convicted in March 2023 and sentenced June 29, 2023.

**Evidence:** ADMITTED_AND_TRIAL_PROVEN_EXCHANGE: corporate DPA admissions plus jury conviction, with legislative-service confirmation of the later statutory reversal.

**Public roles and history:** FirstEnergy was a public utility holding company; Householder used the enterprise to build support for his speakership. Campaign strategist Jeffrey Longstreth and lobbyist Juan Cespedes pleaded guilty, according to DOJ's 2023 account.

**Counterevidence and limits:** The nuclear subsidy did not remain permanently secure: HB128 removed it. HB128 was a partial change, so this record does not claim that all HB6 provisions disappeared. Enforcement and repeal limit any inference of complete or irreversible corporate control. The nearly US$61 million is a conspiracy aggregate, not a per-vote price. Corporate admissions do not by themselves establish every executive's individual guilt. This bounded review reports the dated trial, agreement and legislation rather than asserting that every later appeal or proceeding has ended.

Sources: [DOJ, FirstEnergy charged federally, agrees to terms of deferred prosecution settlement, July 22, 2021](https://www.justice.gov/usao-sdoh/pr/firstenergy-charged-federally-agrees-terms-deferred-prosecution-settlement) — Corporate admissions, 501(c)(4) route, specific official action and separate US$230 million penalty; [DOJ, Former Ohio House Speaker sentenced to 20 years in prison, June 29, 2023](https://www.justice.gov/usao-sdoh/pr/former-ohio-house-speaker-sentenced-20-years-prison-leading-racketeering-conspiracy) — Nearly US$61 million, March 2017–March 2020 period, Generation Now, passage and referendum, jury verdict and sentence; [Ohio Legislative Service Commission, HB128 Final Analysis, April 21, 2021](https://www.legislature.ohio.gov/download?format=pdf&key=16379) — As Passed by the General Assembly; summary and Payments for nuclear resources; effective June 30, 2021.

## Continuity that the evidence can establish

The 1787 debates explicitly connect plantation and shipping interests to constitutional bargaining. Post-emancipation penal labor records document a different legal and financial mechanism. These are concrete institutional connections; they do not establish a single lineage of people controlling all later policy. A family name, school, friendship or shared institution requires a documented financial or decision-making link before it supports an influence claim. ## Limits and next evidence

No global spending total or percentage of U.S. policy controlled by wealth is calculated. Historical currencies, public debt, securities, settlements and bribes cannot be added into a meaningful checkbook total. The case selection favors strong documentary chains and therefore cannot estimate how common corrupt influence is. Missing periods, transactions, biographies and private archives remain gaps rather than zeros.

A deeper investigation should extend each documented chain with original payment records, lobbying instructions, recipients, precise provisions, implementation and competing explanations. Current public records can support that work; private lives, childhood anecdotes and ancestry alone do not establish policy intent. Readers can challenge a claim by identifying its record ID, conflicting source and the exact correction.
